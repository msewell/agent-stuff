# Pronunciation and non-English text

## When pronunciation helps

Include pronunciation only when readers are likely to need it to
identify or discuss the subject. Do not add it to every name, title, or
place merely because a transcription exists. Keep pronunciation near the
first useful occurrence and use one consistent system within a page.

Use the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) when the supplied page or
local convention provides it. Prefer a broad transcription for ordinary
English names unless a finer distinction is essential to the subject. Do
not invent an IPA transcription from spelling alone.

Give a plain-English pronunciation respelling only when it materially
helps the intended reader and does not imply a false precision. Keep any
respelling short, readable, and tied to the relevant English variety. Do
not mix a casual respelling and an unexplained technical transcription
in the same phrase.

Use inline prose for a supplied pronunciation because strict Markdown
provides no dedicated pronunciation template:

``` markdown
The name is pronounced /ˈɛɡzæmpəl/.
```

Do not place a pronunciation in a heading. Do not use a code block for
IPA, and do not use invisible markup, hover text, or custom attributes
to carry pronunciation data.

## Other languages

Use English prose as the page's main language. Include non-English text
only when it identifies the subject, provides a necessary original form,
clarifies a translation, or is itself the object of discussion. Do not
collect alternative-language forms without a reader purpose.

Give an English translation or explanation for a non-English term unless
the meaning is obvious from context. Preserve the source language's
spelling, capitalization, and diacritics when they are part of the word
or name. Do not replace a standard English form with an unfamiliar
native-language form solely to appear precise.

Use an established romanization or transliteration when supplied by the
page, local guide, or reliable material. Do not mix romanization systems
in a page. When multiple forms are necessary, identify their languages
or systems in ordinary prose.

Do not italicize proper names, place names, organization names, or
non-Latin-script text merely because they are not English. Italicize
isolated foreign words and phrases that have not become ordinary
English, except where local usage clearly treats them as unitalicized
terms.

## Translation and quotation

Make clear whether text is an original-language quotation, a
translation, or a transliteration. Preserve the original wording of a
supplied translation when quoting it. Do not present a machine-generated
or editor-invented translation as an authoritative quotation.

Translate for comprehension, not word-for-word display. If a literal
translation would mislead, use idiomatic English and explain a
significant ambiguity in prose. Do not use a foreign term as a
substitute for a precise English explanation.

When a translated title is used as an English title, follow the supplied
English capitalization and title formatting. Preserve the original
title's capitalization when the original form itself is shown. Do not
apply English title case automatically to a non-English title.

## Diacritics, ligatures, and characters

Retain a diacritic when it is part of the established spelling of a
proper name or foreign term. Do not add or remove diacritics
inconsistently. Use the form most readers will recognize when supplied
local usage establishes a different English spelling.

Use ligatures in a language where they are standard. Do not use archaic
ligatures in ordinary English prose. Preserve a ligature in a proper
name or direct quotation when it is significant to identification or
meaning.

Use a real Unicode character for a meaningful letter, symbol, or
diacritic when it is portable and clear. Do not insert an invisible
character, a private-use character, or a character that merely imitates
a different letter. Rewrite when a character is likely to be misread or
cannot be represented clearly in strict Markdown.
