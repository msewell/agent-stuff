# Consistency and editorial process

## Preserve a coherent page

Before changing a page, identify its established choices: English
variety, spelling, date format, punctuation, capitalization,
terminology, number style, heading form, and level of formality.
Preserve these choices when they are coherent and accessible.

Do not make a page internally inconsistent in the name of a personal
preference. A change from one acceptable style to another needs a clear
reader benefit, such as correcting an error, removing ambiguity, or
aligning a genuinely mixed page. Do not normalize direct quotations,
proper names, or titles of works to match the surrounding page.

When a page is inconsistent, resolve the inconsistency deliberately.
Prefer the form used in its substantial established prose. If no form is
established, use the defaults in this skill and apply them throughout
the new or rewritten material.

## Make targeted edits

Make the smallest change that resolves the identified problem. Do not
rewrite a sound passage merely to impose a different voice or show a
stylistic preference. Preserve meaning, scope, attribution, and relevant
qualifications.

Separate a factual problem from a style problem. Flag a factual claim
that is unclear, unsupported by supplied material, or internally
contradictory; do not silently invent a correction. Fix a style problem
directly when the intended meaning is clear.

Do not remove a qualification, date, source attribution, or exception
merely to make prose shorter. Remove repetition, filler, and decorative
wording only when the remaining sentence still expresses the intended
scope.

## Resolve ambiguity

Prefer wording that has one likely reading. Define a technical term
before relying on it. Replace an ambiguous pronoun with a noun. Replace
a crowded parenthesis, slash, or dash with a complete sentence when it
carries a necessary distinction.

Do not use a vague time word, comparative adjective, or relative
location when a specific statement is available. Replace “recent”,
“nearby”, “large”, “many”, “significant”, and “often” with a date,
place, number, criterion, or attribution when supplied material provides
one.

Use a page's title and headings to clarify scope, not to hide ambiguity.
A heading should tell readers what material follows; it should not make
a claim that the section does not support.

## Review order

For a full-page review, check these questions in order:

1. Does the lead identify and summarize the subject for a general
    reader?
2. Does the body progress through coherent sections without gaps,
    duplicates, or catch-all material?
3. Are claims, opinions, quotations, and uncertainty stated in the
    appropriate voice?
4. Are language, terminology, dates, numbers, and formatting
    consistent?
5. Are lists, links, images, and code examples necessary and
    accessible?
6. Is every emitted construct valid strict Markdown?

Report a finding only when it changes reader comprehension, accuracy of
presentation, accessibility, maintainability, or valid rendering. Pair
every finding with a concrete replacement or action.

## Common review failures

Do not confuse a page's subject with the page itself. Remove “this page
explains”, “the section below”, “see the image at right”, and similar
self-references. State the explanation directly.

Do not solve a long page by hiding material or adding decorative layout.
Improve structure, split unrelated material into clear sections, or
shorten repetition. Do not solve a dense paragraph by converting every
clause into a list.

Do not treat stylistic conformity as permission to change source
meaning. A quotation, proper name, historical form, or supplied title
may legitimately differ from the surrounding page.

Do not report inferred conventions as a review result. Use them to guide
edits; return only the reader-facing findings and specific
recommendations.
