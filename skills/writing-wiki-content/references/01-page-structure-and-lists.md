## Purpose, title, and lead

Give the page one clear subject and scope. Write for a reader who
arrives without prior context. The title should identify the subject
concisely; do not make it a sentence, a question, a slogan, or a
container for unrelated topics.

Begin with a lead: a concise summary that identifies the subject,
establishes its context, and states the most important points. The lead
should stand on its own as an accessible overview of the page. Do not
divide it with headings, duplicate the title, or overload it with
details that the body explains better.

Give a short page only the structure it needs. Do not add headings
merely to imitate a longer page. For a long page, summarize the central
topic first and develop the subject from broad context to detail,
exceptions, and consequences.

Do not make claims about the page itself, its place in a wiki, its
navigation, or what a reader can see or do. State the underlying fact
instead.

## Sections

Divide the body into sections only when the division helps readers find
and understand substantial parts of the topic. Each section should cover
one coherent aspect of the subject. Put material in an order that a
general reader can follow:

1. context and definitions needed to understand the subject
2. the subject's central characteristics or history
3. detail, variation, examples, and consequences

Avoid a section that contains only one short paragraph unless the page
needs it for clear navigation. Merge a very short section with a related
section, or expand it only with relevant material. Do not create a
section named “Miscellaneous”, “Other”, “Trivia”, or “Notes” as a
catch-all for facts that do not fit the explanation.

Use paragraphs to develop one main idea at a time. Begin with the point,
supply the explanation or evidence, and end before the subject changes.
Do not use one-sentence paragraphs repeatedly, but do not combine
unrelated ideas into a dense block.

## Headings

Use sentence case for headings unless a proper name or ordinary prose
convention requires capitals. Prefer concise nouns and noun phrases:

``` markdown
## Early life

## Publication history
```

Do not number, letter, phrase as a question, or decorate headings. Do
not repeat the page subject in a lower-level heading when the
surrounding hierarchy already supplies it. Avoid terminal punctuation,
links, emphasis, code, images, and quotation marks in headings.

Use a sequential hierarchy. If the page title is included in the
document, use one `#` heading for it and begin sections with `##`.
Otherwise begin with `##`. Never skip a level merely to make a heading
look smaller.

Keep headings unique and stable. Rename one only when clarity outweighs
the risk of breaking links to that section. Do not use bold text, a list
item, or a sentence fragment as a substitute for a heading.

## Lists within pages

Use prose when it explains a sequence, cause, qualification, or
relationship clearly. Use a list when readers need to scan distinct
items, compare like items, follow a procedure, or see a complete set.

Use an unordered list by default. Use an ordered list only when the
order is essential, when items will be referred to by number, or when
the numbering has independent meaning. Do not number items merely to
make a list appear more formal.

Introduce a list with a complete sentence or a clear lead-in. Keep the
items parallel in grammar, scope, and level of detail. Do not mix
complete sentences, sentence fragments, questions, and headings in one
list.

- Write complete-sentence items in sentence case with final periods.
- Write fragment items consistently, usually after a lead-in ending with
  a colon.
- Do not leave blank lines between simple items.
- Avoid a list with one item unless its role as a list is clear.
- Do not nest lists deeply. Rewrite or divide the material when the
  hierarchy becomes difficult to scan.

Do not use a list to accumulate loosely related facts. Integrate such
facts into explanatory prose or give each a meaningful section. Do not
use a list where the order, grouping, or inclusion criteria are unclear.

## Stand-alone lists

A page whose primary purpose is a list still needs a concise lead.
Define what the list includes, its scope, the meaning of its ordering,
and any important limitation. Use a stable, reader-relevant ordering
such as chronological, alphabetical, geographical, or by a clearly
stated criterion.

Give entries enough context to distinguish them. Keep entry format
consistent. Do not mix a detailed paragraph for one entry with a bare
name for another unless the difference is meaningful and explained.
Split an overlong list into sections with descriptive headings rather
than hiding or collapsing material.

## Appendices and related material

Keep the explanatory body separate from supplementary reading and
outbound resources. If a page includes a short list of related pages or
external resources, put it after the body, use a descriptive heading,
and include only items that are directly useful. Do not create a
directory of every tangential resource.
