## General principles

Use punctuation to show a sentence's structure and meaning, not to
imitate speech or add drama. Keep a page's established punctuation style
unless it is unclear or incorrect. Use one space after terminal
punctuation.

Use standard Unicode punctuation directly when it is available and
unambiguous. Do not insert HTML entities, invisible control characters,
or nonportable spacing markup. When a special character would make a
page difficult to read or edit, rewrite instead.

## Apostrophes and possessives

Use an apostrophe for possession and for standard contractions only.
Form a singular possessive with `'s` in ordinary prose, including most
singular names ending in *s*: “the witness's account”. Form a regular
plural possessive with an apostrophe after the *s*: “the witnesses'
accounts”.

Do not use an apostrophe to form a simple plural: write “the 1990s”,
“three URLs”, and “two MPs”, not “1990's”, “URL's”, or “MP's”. Retain an
unusual apostrophe only when it is part of a proper name, title, or
direct quotation.

## Quotation marks

Use double quotation marks for direct quotations and titles of short
works. Use single quotation marks only for a quotation within a
quotation or where an established local style requires them. Do not use
quotation marks for emphasis, slang, a translation, or an ordinary
descriptive label.

Place punctuation according to the sentence's grammar and the
established English variety. When no local practice is inferable, place
a comma or period inside quotation marks only if it belongs to the
quoted material or is required by the chosen American default; place
other punctuation according to whether it belongs to the quotation.

Do not use a quotation mark to distance the page from a word or imply
that a fact is doubtful. Attribute the opinion or explain the disputed
usage instead.

## Parentheses and brackets

Use parentheses for a short clarification, alternative name,
pronunciation, date, or exception that is helpful but not central to the
sentence. Do not use nested parentheses. If a parenthetical grows into a
sentence or carries essential reasoning, integrate it into the prose or
make it a separate sentence.

Use square brackets only inside a quotation for a necessary editorial
clarification, alteration, or note. Do not use brackets as decorative
punctuation or as a substitute for a parenthesis in ordinary prose.

Avoid slash constructions when a conjunction, preposition, or rewrite is
clearer. A slash is appropriate for a well-established compact form, a
path, a ratio, or an alternative that readers genuinely need to see
together. Do not write “and/or” unless both alternatives together are
essential and cannot be stated more precisely.

## Commas, colons, and semicolons

Use commas to separate items, clauses, and introductory material where
they prevent misreading. Follow the page's established serial-comma
practice. If no practice is established, use a serial comma when it
prevents ambiguity; otherwise prefer a sentence that does not depend on
the choice.

Use a colon after a complete clause to introduce an explanation, list,
example, or quotation. Do not put a space before a colon. Do not use
multiple colons in one sentence unless a technical expression requires
them.

Use a semicolon to join closely related independent clauses or to
separate complex list items that already contain commas. Do not use it
where a full stop, comma, colon, or rewrite is clearer. Do not join
independent clauses with a comma alone.

## Hyphens and dashes

Use a hyphen in a compound modifier when it prevents ambiguity: “a
high-speed train”, “a two-year plan”, “a well-known result”. Do not
hyphenate an adverb ending in *-ly* with the adjective it modifies: “a
newly available edition”. Do not insert a hyphen into a proper name.

Use an en dash for a range when the page already uses it consistently:
`2019–2024`, `pages 12–18`. Use *to* when a range character may not
render reliably or when the relation is verbal rather than numeric. Do
not use a hyphen as a substitute for an en dash in a range.

Use an em dash sparingly for a strong break or interruption. Do not use
it as a substitute for ordinary commas, parentheses, colons, or sentence
division. Preserve the page's spacing style around em dashes; when no
style exists, use an unspaced em dash only if it improves clarity.

Use an en dash, not a hyphen, when linking two equal entities in a
relationship: “a London–Paris route”. Rewrite if that distinction would
distract more than it helps.

## Ellipses and terminal punctuation

Use an ellipsis to show an actual omission from quoted material or a
deliberate trailing-off in quoted speech. Do not use it to create
suspense, replace a full stop, or conceal a change in meaning. Do not
add an ellipsis at the beginning or end of a quotation merely because it
has been extracted from a longer passage.

End complete sentences with a period, question mark, or exclamation
point as their meaning requires. Avoid exclamation points in
encyclopedic prose except in a direct quotation or name. Do not use
repeated punctuation such as `!!`, `?!`, or `...!`.

Use a question mark for a genuine question, not for a rhetorical
heading. Write a statement when the purpose is explanatory.

## Spacing and line breaks

Do not force line breaks, insert multiple spaces for layout, or rely on
a particular screen width. Strict Markdown has no portable
nonbreaking-space control. Rewrite a phrase that would become confusing
when broken across lines rather than adding raw HTML or an entity.

Do not use indentation for visual decoration. In Markdown, leading
spaces can create a code block; use a block quote only for an actual
quotation and a list only for actual list items.
