## Sentence construction

Write complete, grammatical sentences unless a list, heading, label, or direct quotation properly uses a fragment. Make the subject, verb, and object clear. Put the main point early enough that readers do not need to hold a long opening qualification in memory.

Prefer concrete verbs to forms of *to be* followed by an abstract noun when the concrete verb says the same thing. Write “The committee decided” rather than “The committee made a decision”. Prefer “used” to “made use of”, “because” to “due to the fact that”, and “although” to “despite the fact that”.

Avoid noun strings that force readers to infer relationships: rewrite “regional transport policy development process” as a sentence or a clearer noun phrase. Use a hyphen only when it resolves a real ambiguity in a compound modifier.

Keep modifiers next to the word they modify. Rewrite a dangling or misplaced modifier rather than relying on a reader to infer the intended subject. Avoid “respectively” unless the pairing is short and unmistakable.

Do not use a comma to join independent clauses. Use a period, semicolon, or conjunction with a comma as the relationship requires. Do not use a semicolon where a simple full stop makes two complete ideas easier to read.

## Verbs and agreement

Make a verb agree with its grammatical subject, not with a nearby noun. For a collective noun, use singular or plural consistently according to whether the group acts as one unit or as individuals. Rewrite when a collective construction becomes awkward.

Use the active voice when it identifies a relevant actor and makes the action direct. Use the passive voice when the actor is unknown, unimportant, obvious, or when the page should keep attention on the affected subject. Do not treat passive voice as an error by itself.

Use past tense for completed events. Use present tense for enduring facts, current states, and the contents of a work. Use present perfect for an action that began in the past and remains relevant. Keep a narrative's tense stable unless its time frame changes.

Use *may* for permission or possibility, *might* for a more tentative possibility, *can* for ability, and *must* for a genuine requirement. Do not use *would* where a simple past or present verb says what actually happened.

## Pronouns and people

Use pronouns with a clear antecedent. Repeat the relevant noun when *it*, *this*, *they*, *he*, *she*, or *which* could refer to more than one thing. Do not let *this* or *that* stand alone for an entire prior argument; write “this change”, “that finding”, or a more precise noun.

Use singular *they* when it is the clearest gender-neutral reference to an unspecified person. Use a person's stated or supplied pronouns when writing about that person. Do not use generic *he* for an unspecified reader, worker, student, or person.

Avoid first-person singular and plural voice unless the page genuinely speaks for a named author or group. Avoid addressing readers as *you* or *your*. Replace “you can calculate” with “the value can be calculated” or a specific subject.

Use *who* for people and *that* or *which* for things according to the page's established style. Rewrite a restrictive or nonrestrictive clause when the distinction is likely to distract or mislead.

## Plurals and parallelism

Use a plural noun with *many*, *few*, *several*, and a number greater than one. Use singular with *each*, *every*, *either*, *neither*, and *one of* when the grammar requires it. Rewrite an exception rather than producing a strained agreement.

Keep coordinated items parallel. If a sentence begins “The process includes collecting, reviewing, and…”, complete it with another *-ing* form. If list items begin with verbs, do not change to nouns halfway through. Parallel grammar helps readers compare items without re-parsing each one.

## Word choice

Prefer a precise word to a broad one. Name the relevant country, organization, discipline, event, or group rather than using a vague umbrella term. Do not substitute an unfamiliar formal word for a common one merely to sound authoritative.

Avoid clichés and stock phrasing when a direct statement is available. Avoid mixed metaphors, figurative language that makes a technical claim less precise, and vague intensifiers such as “very”, “extremely”, or “highly” when a measurable description is available.

Avoid euphemism when it conceals an important fact. Avoid inflammatory language when it supplies judgment instead of information. Use the terms supplied by the page's evidence and explain a contested term rather than silently choosing a side.

Do not write “alleged”, “so-called”, “purported”, “supposedly”, or “claimed” as a substitute for clear attribution. If the status of an assertion matters, state who made it, what evidence is supplied, and what is uncertain.

## Technical language

Introduce a technical term when it is more precise than a general alternative, then define it in ordinary English or show its role through a short example. Do not make readers learn a chain of prerequisites before they can understand the page's main point.

Avoid explaining familiar material at a level too elementary for the page's audience, but do not assume specialized knowledge without signaling it. If a prerequisite needs a full treatment, link to a supplied explanatory page or add a concise background section.

## Instructions and self-reference

Do not tell readers to “remember”, “note”, “consider”, “see”, “click”, or “look above”. State the relevant fact directly. Avoid “it should be noted that”, “it is important to note that”, and similar phrases; delete them unless they add actual information.

Do not refer to the page's layout, section number, links, images, search function, readers, editors, or future updates. Write the content so that it remains meaningful when rendered, printed, translated, excerpted, or read with assistive technology.
