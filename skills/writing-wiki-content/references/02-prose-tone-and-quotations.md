## General-reader prose

Write so that a general reader can understand the page without specialist training. Use direct, precise, and economical English. Prefer familiar words when they carry the same meaning. Minimize jargon and explain a necessary technical term at first use.

Do not introduce specialized words merely to teach them when a widely understood alternative will do. When a prerequisite concept cannot be explained briefly, add a short background explanation before relying on it. Keep the focus on the page subject rather than on the technical process of explanation.

Use active or passive voice according to what keeps the subject and action clear. Do not force active voice when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or would distract from the subject. Avoid sentence openings that delay the main point with long qualifications or empty framing.

Use a formal, explanatory register. Avoid conversational filler, contractions, rhetorical questions, direct commands, and second-person address. Replace “you can see” or “note that” with a direct statement of the fact.

For grammar, pronouns, agreement, and technical language, use the dedicated grammar reference.

## Neutral point of view in prose

Present facts, interpretations, and opinions in the right voice. State an uncontroversial fact directly. Attribute a judgment, prediction, interpretation, or emotive description to the person or source that makes it.

Do not tell readers that something is interesting, important, surprising, controversial, ironic, obvious, or noteworthy. Give the facts that could support that conclusion and let readers decide. Do not use a quotation merely to give a routine statement artificial emphasis.

Avoid editorial language:

- **Puffery:** “world-class”, “leading”, “renowned”, “iconic”, “visionary”, “landmark”, or similar praise without a precise attributed basis.
- **Unsupported superlatives:** “the best”, “the most significant”, “the first”, or “the only” without supplied evidence.
- **Weasel wording:** “some say”, “it is believed”, “many consider”, “widely regarded”, or an unnamed passive claim.
- **Editorializing:** “fortunately”, “regrettably”, “despite this”, “it is worth noting”, “of course”, “clearly”, or “actually”.
- **Scare quotes:** quotation marks around ordinary descriptions to imply doubt, sarcasm, or disapproval.
- **Euphemism or loaded labels:** wording that hides, exaggerates, or prejudges the relevant fact.

Replace vague evaluations with specific information. Replace an unnamed claim with an attributed statement if the supplied material identifies a source. If neither is possible, omit the claim.

Use precise terms for people and groups. Do not use a broad geographical, cultural, racial, religious, or political label when a more specific description is available. Use gender-neutral language when clarity and precision allow it. Do not alter direct quotations, titles of works, or proper names merely to normalize wording.

## Quotations

Quote only when the original wording itself matters: for a distinctive formulation, a precise statement, an opinion, a historical record, or wording under discussion. Otherwise paraphrase accurately and concisely. Most explanatory prose should be in the page's own words.

Reproduce direct quotations faithfully. Preserve wording, spelling, capitalization, dialect, and meaning. Do not silently omit words that change the point, weaken a qualification, or alter the apparent opinion. Use an ellipsis only for a genuine omission, not to create drama or conceal context.

Use square brackets only for a necessary editorial clarification, grammatical adjustment, or expansion that readers need to understand the quotation. Keep the change minimal. Do not use brackets to rewrite a source's opinion or to correct an error that matters to its meaning.

Use quotation marks for a short quotation. Use a block quote for a long quotation, a quotation with more than one paragraph, or one that needs visual separation. Introduce a block quotation in prose and identify the speaker or source when supplied.

```markdown
The report concluded:

> The service will remain available during the migration.
```

Do not put quotation marks around a block quote. Do not use italics merely to mark quoted material. Preserve italic emphasis that appears in the supplied quotation; if the editor adds emphasis, state that plainly in brackets when the distinction matters.

Attribute an opinion in the surrounding prose. Prefer “The report described the change as costly” to “The change was ‘costly’.” Do not attach a loaded verb such as “admitted”, “boasted”, or “claimed” unless the supplied material justifies it; “said”, “wrote”, “reported”, or a specific neutral verb is usually clearer.

Do not use direct quotation to preserve decorative capitalization, unusual spacing, colored text, or branding style. Normalize purely typographic details when doing so does not change meaning. Do not change the English variety inside a direct quotation.
