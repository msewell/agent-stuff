## Sentence case

Use sentence case for page titles, section headings, list items, and
ordinary prose. Capitalize the first word and any proper names. Do not
use title case merely because a heading is prominent.

``` markdown
## Development of the public library

- First published edition
- Later translations
```

Do not use capital letters for emphasis. If a word needs emphasis,
rewrite the sentence or use limited italics. Do not use all capitals for
a title, warning, label, or abbreviation expansion unless it is part of
the supplied proper name.

## Proper and generic names

Capitalize a unique, formally named person, place, institution,
organization, event, work, law, treaty, species name where a supplied
scientific convention requires it, language, nationality, religion, or
product name. Use lower case for a generic category, description, role,
office, or object.

Write “the ministry”, “the company”, “the river”, “the university”, and
“the president” in lower case when they are generic references.
Capitalize them when they are part of an official name: “the Ministry of
Finance”, “River Thames”, “the University of Northbridge”, “President
Rivera”.

Capitalize a title immediately before a personal name when the title
functions as part of the name in supplied usage. Use lower case when it
is descriptive: “the president spoke” and “President Rivera spoke”.
Avoid honorifics unless they are necessary to identify the person or are
part of a direct quotation.

Capitalize a geographical direction only when it is part of a recognized
regional name. Write “north of the city” but “Northern Europe” when the
latter is the established regional term. Use lower case for seasons,
academic subjects, political ideologies, and common names unless a
proper name rule applies.

Do not capitalize a common term merely because a brand, organization,
movement, or advocate capitalizes it. Do not decapitalize a supplied
proper name merely because it resembles a common word.

## Titles and the word “the”

Use the ordinary capitalization of a title of a work, official
publication, organization, or named event. Retain the capitalization of
a modern work's title. Do not capitalize *the* before a proper name
unless it is inseparable from the name or title.

Use an initial capital after a colon only when the following material is
a complete sentence, a proper name, or the established page style
requires it. Use lower case after a colon when it introduces a fragment,
list, or continuation.

## Acronyms and initialisms

An abbreviation shortens a word or phrase. An initialism uses initial
letters and is pronounced letter by letter. An acronym is pronounced as
a word. In practical page writing, apply the same reader-first rule to
all three.

Write an unfamiliar abbreviation in full on first useful occurrence,
followed by the short form in parentheses. Do not expand it repeatedly.
Do not expand a widely understood term when doing so slows the prose
more than it helps.

``` markdown
The World Health Organization (WHO) published the report.

The WHO updated the guidance in 2025.
```

Use capitals in the short form only where the established abbreviation
uses capitals. Do not capitalize every word in the expanded form merely
because the abbreviation is capitalized. Do not add periods inside an
initialism unless the supplied page has an established style that
requires them.

Do not devise a new abbreviation or acronym. Do not shorten a name if
the shortened form could refer to multiple subjects. Avoid an
abbreviation when it appears only once, interrupts a short page, or
sounds informal.

## Common abbreviations

Use an established abbreviation only when it is likely to be understood
by the intended reader. Spell out an unfamiliar title, place,
organization, or Latin expression. Avoid legalistic, bureaucratic, or
note-like shortening in ordinary prose.

Keep a page's period style consistent: do not alternate between forms
such as `Dr` and `Dr.` or `U.S.` and `US` without a reason. Retain the
exact form in direct quotations, titles, and proper names.

Use *circa* in prose when approximation matters and space permits. Avoid
invented forms such as “approx.” in ordinary sentences. Use *and* rather
than `&` in ordinary prose; retain `&` in a proper name, title, or
supplied quotation.

Use symbols for standard units and established mathematical notation
when readers can understand them from context. Spell out a symbol's
meaning at first use if it is uncommon, subject-specific, or ambiguous.
