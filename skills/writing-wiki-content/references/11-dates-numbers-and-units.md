## General consistency

Retain the page's established date, time, number, and unit formats. Keep
comparable values in the same form. Do not reformat dates, numbers, or
units inside direct quotations, titles, proper names, or supplied source
text unless a purely typographic change is necessary and does not alter
meaning.

When no format is established, choose an unambiguous form and use it
consistently. Avoid all-numeric dates because `03/04/2026` has more than
one ordinary reading. Use the American default only when needed:
`March 4, 2026`.

## Dates and periods

Write a full date as `March 4, 2026` under the default style. Write a
month and year as `March 2026`; do not add a comma. Write a year alone
as `2026`. Keep month names spelled out when a numeric date could be
ambiguous.

Use a specific date or period rather than “currently”, “today”,
“recently”, “soon”, “now”, or “at present”, unless the surrounding
material gives a durable reference date. Write “as of March 2026” only
when that qualification is necessary and supplied.

Use an en dash for a numeric range when the page uses it consistently:
`2019–2024`, `March–May 2026`. Otherwise use *to*: `from 2019 to 2024`.
Do not write `from 2019–2024` or `between 2019–2024`; pair *from* with
*to* and *between* with *and*.

Write decades as `the 1990s`, not `the 1990's`. Write centuries in words
when that is clearer: “the 21st century”. Avoid ambiguous shorthand such
as `'90s` unless the local style and context make the century
unmistakable.

Use seasons only when they are part of a natural cycle or a cited title.
For a dated event, a month or date is usually clearer because seasons
differ between hemispheres.

## Time

Use a time format consistently within the page. Under the default style,
use `3:15 PM` or a 24-hour time where the surrounding subject requires
it. Include a time zone when readers could otherwise misinterpret the
time. Use a named zone or UTC offset supplied by the source; do not
invent one.

Use `noon` and `midnight` only when they are less ambiguous than a
numeric time. Do not write `12 AM` or `12 PM` when a reader could
mistake which one is meant.

## Numbers in prose

Spell out a number at the beginning of a sentence. Elsewhere, choose
words or figures for readability and keep comparable quantities
consistent. Use figures for measurements, precise values, dates,
statistics, percentages, ratios, dimensions, and values that readers
need to compare quickly.

Do not mix words and figures for similar quantities in the same context:
write either “three, four, and five participants” or “3, 4, and 5
participants”. Rewrite a sentence that begins with an awkward large
figure rather than beginning with the numeral.

Use digits in a range, a table-like sequence expressed in prose, or a
technical measurement. Spell out an approximate, simple quantity when
precision is not the point: “about a hundred examples”. Do not use a
false exactness such as `100.0` when the evidence supports only `100`.

Use commas to group long numbers under the American default: `1,500`,
`25,000`, `1,000,000`. Keep a page's existing grouping convention if it
is coherent. Do not add separators to identifiers, years, page numbers,
or numerical codes.

## Fractions, percentages, and ratios

Use a simple fraction in words when it reads naturally: “one-half”,
“two-thirds”. Use figures when comparison or calculation matters: `1/2`,
`2/3`, `0.75`. Do not use Unicode fraction glyphs if they may be
confused or render inconsistently.

Write a percentage as a figure followed by `%`: `12%`. Do not insert a
space before the percent sign unless an established local convention
requires it. Make clear what quantity is the denominator when it is not
obvious.

Write a ratio with a colon when it is a literal ratio: `3:1`. In
ordinary explanation, use words when they are clearer: “a ratio of three
to one”. Do not use a slash where it could mean a ratio, alternative, or
division.

## Units and currencies

Use a space between a figure and a unit: `17 kg`, `5 MB`, `20 °C`,
`6 km`. Do not pluralize a unit symbol: `5 kg`, not `5 kgs`. Use the
same unit system and precision for comparable values unless the supplied
material needs both.

Name a currency when a bare symbol is ambiguous: `US$20`, `C$20`, or
`20 Canadian dollars`. Use the same currency form throughout a page. Do
not silently convert historical or foreign values; identify the
conversion's date, basis, and approximation only when supplied.

Give a conversion when it materially helps the intended reader, but do
not overload prose with duplicate units. Preserve the original value and
make the conversion clearly secondary. Do not imply a conversion is
exact when exchange rates, measurement standards, or historical values
make it approximate.

Use symbols such as `×`, `±`, `≤`, and `≥` only when the intended
readers will understand them and the surrounding content makes their
role clear. Otherwise write the relation in words.
